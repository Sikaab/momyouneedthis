Childcare Costs by State Average Infant & Preschool Center Prices, State by State

Childcare is the bill that quietly decides everything else — whether a second income actually pays, when a sibling makes sense, and what "affordable" even means where you live. So we put the numbers in one place: the average price of full-time center care in every state, for an infant and a 4-year-old side by side, plus the two comparisons that matter most — what care costs next to housing, and what share of a family's income it swallows. Find your state in the table, then read the three findings underneath it. The two-kids math is the one that surprises most parents.

💗 MomYouNeedThis • Last updated: November 6, 2026

Quick Answer

In 2025, full-time infant care in a childcare center cost $27,067 a year in Massachusetts — the most expensive state in Child Care Aware of America's 2025 data — and $6,492 a year in Mississippi, the cheapest. And the bill for two children in a center costs more than a full year of housing in 38 of the 46 states that reported prices.

At a Glance

  • Most expensive infant care: Massachusetts — $27,067 a year (Child Care Aware of America, 2025)
  • Least expensive infant care: Mississippi — $6,492 a year
  • Middle state: $15,080 a year for infant center care (median of the 46 reported state prices)
  • Two children in a center: Costs more than a year of housing in 38 of 46 states
  • Care vs. college: Infant care beats in-state public tuition in 38 of 46 states
  • The 7% affordability rule: Only Mississippi (6%) comes in under it for married couples
  • The data: Child Care Aware of America 2025 prices for 46 states; EPI figures fill in 5 more, flagged in the table

💰 Two Kids in a Center Can Cost More Than Your House

One child in care is a big bill. Two is a different financial universe. Adding an infant and a 4-year-old together, the yearly center bill beats a full year of housing costs in 38 of the 46 states that reported 2025 prices to Child Care Aware of America.

Massachusetts is the extreme: $48,431 a year for two children in a center, against $32,712 for housing — the daycare bill is nearly half again the housing bill. Washington, D.C. is higher still on the Economic Policy Institute's figures, at $53,686 a year for two children in a center. Only eight states flip the order — Alabama, Alaska, Georgia, Idaho, Mississippi, New Jersey, South Dakota and Texas — and in most of them it is close.

This is the math behind the decision so many moms run at the kitchen table: not "can we afford daycare?" but "does my paycheck survive it?" When two kids in care out-cost the mortgage or rent, working less, going part-time, or waiting to space siblings stops looking like a lifestyle choice and starts looking like arithmetic. Neither answer is wrong — but it helps to run your own state's two numbers, side by side, before anyone tells you what you "should" do.

🎓 Infant Care Costs More Than College in Most States

Here is the comparison that lands hardest at baby showers: in 38 of the 46 reporting states, one year of infant care in a center costs more than one year of in-state public college tuition. Put two children in a center and care beats tuition in all 46.

Florida shows the gap plainly — a year of infant care runs $14,230, which is 2.2 times a year of public college tuition ($6,359). And there is a catch that makes the childcare version harder than the college version: college arrives after eighteen years of saving, tax-advantaged accounts and financial aid forms. Infant care arrives this year, at full price, right when parents are at the lowest-earning, most sleep-deprived point of their careers.

None of this means college got cheap. It means the most expensive "tuition" most families ever pay is the one nobody calls tuition — and it is due before your baby can walk.

📏 The 7% Affordability Rule — and the One State That Meets It

The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services has an official line for this: childcare counts as affordable when it takes no more than 7% of family income. Measured against median married-couple income, exactly one of the 46 reporting states comes in under that line — Mississippi, at 6%. South Dakota sits right on it at 7%. Every other reporting state is over, and Hawaii is furthest over at 18%.

For single parents, the picture is starker. Infant center care swallows 57% of median single-parent income in Massachusetts — more than half of everything, before rent — and at least half in five states: Massachusetts (57%), Oregon (52%), Hawaii (51%), New York (51%) and Washington (50%).

So if care feels unaffordable in your state, that is not a budgeting failure. By the federal government's own definition, it is unaffordable in 45 of 46 states for a typical two-parent family, and dramatically so for single moms. The 7% line is useful precisely because it gives parents a neutral yardstick: you can look at your state's percentage in the table above and know whether the problem is your budget — or the price.

🔍 Method & Sources

Primary source — Child Care Aware of America (2025)

Prices come from Child Care in America: 2025 Price & Supply (published May 2026; 2025 prices), extracted from CCAoA's own per-state fact sheets — obtained for 46 of 51 jurisdictions. Data © Child Care Aware of America, republished with citation as its reuse terms require.

The five flagged states (†)

Colorado, New Mexico, South Carolina and Washington, D.C. did not report 2025 price data to CCAoA, and no New Hampshire fact sheet was available. Their prices come from the Economic Policy Institute (data updated July 2026), which inflation-adjusts CCAoA's 2024 prices — a different vintage, so those rows are flagged and never blended into a comparison unlabeled. EPI publishes no toddler price; the Age 4 column for † states carries EPI's 4-year-old center price.

How the Age 4 column was built

For CCAoA states, the 4-year-old center price is the fact sheet's two-child center total minus its infant center price — arithmetic on two figures printed on the same sheet. Validated against CCAoA's published 4-year-old prices: Massachusetts ($21,364) and Mississippi ($6,240) match exactly.

About that national average

CCAoA's blended national average is $13,184 a year — and CCAoA itself cautions against using that blended figure as a benchmark for any individual state, so this page doesn't. Our midpoint is the median of the 46 state infant-center prices in the table: $15,080.

Housing and income columns

For CCAoA states, housing is the annual housing cost printed on each fact sheet, and the income percentages use the married-couple and single-parent median incomes printed on the same sheets. For † states, housing is average annual rent (Census Bureau ACS, via EPI) and percentages use EPI's median income. Different measures, deliberately kept in labeled columns.

Alaska (‡)

CCAoA's 2025 fact sheet puts Alaska infant center care at $12,425; EPI puts it at $22,016 — a $9,591 gap, the largest divergence of any state. The table shows the 2025 figure flagged, and Alaska appears in no ranking on this page pending a third check.

FAQ

❓ Childcare Costs FAQ

How much does childcare cost per year?

It depends on the state. Across the 46 states in Child Care Aware of America's 2025 data, the middle price for full-time infant care in a center is $15,080 a year. The range runs from $6,492 a year in Mississippi to $27,067 a year in Massachusetts. Prices for a 4-year-old in a center are lower in every state — $6,240 a year in Mississippi and $21,364 in Massachusetts.

Which state has the most expensive childcare?

Massachusetts, in Child Care Aware of America's 2025 data: $27,067 a year for full-time infant care in a center, and $48,431 for an infant and a 4-year-old together. Washington, D.C. did not report 2025 prices to Child Care Aware; on the Economic Policy Institute's table, which covers all 51 jurisdictions, D.C. is higher at $29,809 for infant care — which is why every 'most expensive' claim on this page names its dataset.

Which state has the cheapest childcare?

Mississippi. Full-time infant care in a center averages $6,492 a year there (Child Care Aware of America, 2025) — less than a quarter of the Massachusetts price — and at 6% of median married-couple income it is also the only state that meets the federal affordability benchmark.

What percentage of income should childcare cost?

The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services sets the affordability benchmark at no more than 7% of family income. Measured against median married-couple income, only Mississippi (6%) comes in under it for infant center care, and South Dakota sits exactly at 7%. Hawaii is the furthest over, at 18%. For single parents the shares are far higher — infant care takes 57% of median single-parent income in Massachusetts.

Is childcare more expensive than college?

In most states, yes. Infant care in a center costs more than a year of in-state public college tuition in 38 of the 46 states in Child Care Aware of America's 2025 data, and care for two children in a center costs more than tuition in all 46. In Florida, one year of infant care ($14,230) costs 2.2 times a year of public tuition ($6,359).

How much does childcare for two children cost?

Two children — an infant and a 4-year-old — in a center costs more than a full year of housing in 38 of the 46 reporting states. Massachusetts is the extreme: $48,431 a year for two children in a center, against $32,712 for housing. On the Economic Policy Institute's figures, two children in a center in Washington, D.C. totals $53,686 a year.
