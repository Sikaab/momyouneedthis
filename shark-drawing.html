✏️ How to Draw a Shark: Step by Step

One step per box — draw each new part in a different color so kids can see exactly what changed.

Step 1: The Body

Draw a long sideways oval, pointier at one end — like a torpedo. This is the shark's body.

Step 2: The Tail Fin

Draw a sideways V or crescent shape at the back end for the tail fin.

Step 3: The Dorsal Fin

Draw a triangle on top of the body for the famous dorsal fin.

Step 4: The Toothy Mouth

Draw a curved smile line near the front and add little triangle teeth along it.

Step 5: Eye and Gills

Add a round eye above the mouth and three curved gill lines on the side.

Step 6: Color It In

Color the top blue-gray and the belly white — real sharks are two-toned!

🦈 Sharks in the Real World

Kids who love drawing sharks usually want the facts too — and sharks have the best facts in the ocean. Sharks have been swimming the oceans since before there were trees — they are older than the dinosaurs, older than Mount Everest, and they have survived nearly everything the planet has thrown at them. A shark's skeleton is made of cartilage, the same bendy material as your ears and nose, which keeps it light and quick in the water.

A shark's teeth are endlessly replaceable: they grow in rows like a conveyor belt, and a shark may use tens of thousands of teeth in its lifetime. Sharks also sense the tiny electrical signals of other animals, a sixth sense people simply do not have. Ask your child whether their shark is a hunter, a cruiser, or the ocean's lifeguard — teeth out either way.

What You'll Need to Draw a Shark

Blue or gray for the body, white for the belly — the two-tone look is what makes a shark read as a shark. Black for the outlines and the eye. The triangle teeth need careful placement along the jaw line, so a crayon that isn't too blunt helps. Light blue paper makes a perfect ocean background.

What They're Learning

Sharks teach triangles in a fun context — the dorsal fin, the tail, and every single tooth. The curved body (a long arc, not a circle or oval) is a new line type that builds confidence with sweeping motions. Placing the eye, gills, and fins in the right spots is also good practice in picturing where parts go before drawing them.

Make It Yours

Drawing sharks your own way starts with the mouth — fierce teeth or a friendly grin changes the whole character. Add an open mouth full of triangle teeth for a fierce shark, or keep the mouth closed for a friendly one. Draw fish swimming away, seaweed on the ocean floor, or a treasure chest nearby. A hammerhead shark (wide flat head) is a fun variation once they've mastered the basic shape. Remoras stuck to the belly are a silly detail kids love.
