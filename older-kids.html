✍️ Handwriting & Cursive

By age five or six, most children can grip a pencil with control and copy simple shapes — and handwriting shifts from scribbles to a real school skill.

This topic starts with cursive, because that is where the biggest gap is: children see cursive on birthday cards, in grandparents' notes and in older siblings' schoolwork, and they want to read and write it themselves. Inside the cursive hub you will find a complete alphabet chart in the traditional style taught in US schools, showing every uppercase and lowercase letter, plus step-by-step guidance on how children actually learn the strokes — which letters to group together, how long to practice, and how to use the chart during homework.

🧭 How to Use These Pages

These pages work best as short, regular practice — a few minutes alongside school, not a second school day at the kitchen table. Here is the approach that gets results without tears.

  1. Start where school is. Match the page to what your child is learning this month. Practice that lines up with classwork feels relevant, sticks faster, and shows your child exactly why the skill matters.
  2. Print one page at a time. A single fresh page feels like an invitation; a thick stack feels like a chore. Printing on demand also means you can reprint a favorite page as many times as your child wants to redo it.
  3. Keep sessions short. Ten to fifteen minutes of focused practice beats an hour of nagging. Stop while it is still going well — ending on a success makes tomorrow's session much easier to start.
  4. Let them check their own work. Comparing their letters against the chart, circling their neatest word, spotting their own wobbly line — self-checking builds independence, and it is the moment practice turns into pride.

A small setup helps more than you would expect. Give your child a pencil that fits their hand — a shorter pencil or a simple grip helps tired fingers — a flat surface at the right height, and decent light. Then keep the finished pages in a folder or binder instead of the recycling bin. Older kids are quietly proud of a growing portfolio, and flipping back to their first page a few months later shows them their own progress more clearly than any praise can.

Not sure whether your child is ready for this section yet? Age alone is a rough guide — readiness shows up in smaller ways first:

  • Writes their own name without being shown the letters
  • Asks how words are spelled or tries to write real words
  • Counts past 20 and understands that numbers stand for amounts
  • Can sit with one task for about ten minutes
  • Calls the preschool pages "babyish" — a classic sign

If some of those sound familiar, start here; if none do yet, the preschool Learning Activities are the right level for now, and this section will be waiting when they are ready. Children move between the two sections at their own pace, and plenty of families use both at once for siblings of different ages.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Are these worksheets really free?

Yes. Every worksheet and activity in the Older Kids section is free to print at home — no signup, no account, no email and no payment. Print as many copies as your own children need.

What ages are the older kids pages for?

They are made for children roughly ages 5 to 8, from kindergarten through grade 3. Every child develops at their own pace, so treat the ages as a guide: if a page feels easy, move up; if it feels hard, it will still be here in a few months.

How is this different from the preschool activities?

The preschool pages teach recognition — what a letter looks like, how many objects are in a group. The older kids pages build fluency: writing letters properly instead of just naming them, reading real words, and solving problems that take more than one step. The pages expect a little more focus, but they are still built to feel like play, not homework.

Do I need to sign up or create an account?

No. Nothing in this section needs an account or an email address. Pick a page, open the printable, and print it.

What should my child start with?

If your child is starting handwriting, begin with the cursive alphabet chart — print it, keep it where they write, and let them explore the letters before any formal practice. If handwriting is already comfortable, watch this page: new topics are added here as they are finished.
