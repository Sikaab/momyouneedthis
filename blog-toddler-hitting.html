🧸 TODDLER BEHAVIOUR

My Toddler Keeps Hitting Me — What Should I Actually Do?

If your toddler has suddenly started hitting, you're probably wondering why it's happening, whether you're handling it wrong and what you're actually supposed to do when they keep hitting even after you say no.

💗 MomYouNeedThis • Practical Parenting Guide
First, take a breath.

Hitting can be a difficult behaviour to deal with, especially when it seems to come out of nowhere. Toddlers are still learning how to handle frustration, communicate what they want and control strong impulses.

That doesn't mean hitting should simply be ignored. The goal is to keep everyone safe, set a clear boundary and help your toddler learn what to do instead.

💡 The Quick Answer

When your toddler hits, keep your response calm, short and predictable.

💗
“I won't let you hit me.”

Then gently create physical space between you and your toddler.

If they try again, repeat the same basic boundary rather than turning the moment into a long lecture.

“I won't let you hit.”

Then create space again.

Why Does My Toddler Keep Hitting?

There isn't always one single reason.

Toddlers may hit when they're:

  • Frustrated
  • Angry
  • Overtired
  • Overwhelmed
  • Excited
  • Trying to get a reaction
  • Struggling to communicate what they want
  • Having difficulty stopping an impulse
  • Testing a boundary

Sometimes parents are especially surprised when their toddler is smiling or laughing while hitting.

That doesn't necessarily mean they think hurting someone is funny. Young children can laugh or smile when they're excited, overwhelmed or unsure how to respond to a situation.

Remember:

The behaviour still needs a clear boundary, but you don't necessarily need to interpret the smile as deliberate cruelty.

What If I Say “No Hitting” And They Do It Again?

This is one of the hardest parts.

You say:

“No hitting.”

They hit again.

You repeat yourself.

They hit again.

Eventually you're wondering whether saying anything is even working.

Instead of continuing to repeat the same sentence over and over, follow the boundary with an action.

1
Set the boundary.

“I won't let you hit me.”

2
Create space.

Move away or create physical distance.

3
Repeat if necessary.

“I won't let you hit.”

Your toddler may test the boundary several times. That doesn't necessarily mean the boundary isn't working. They are learning that hitting consistently results in the same response: the hitting stops and access to your body stops.

What If My Toddler Follows Me And Hits Me Again?

This can feel incredibly overwhelming.

You don't have to stay directly beside your toddler while they're trying to hit you. Your priority is safety.

Create as much space as reasonably possible and remove objects that could hurt someone.

🛑
“I won't let you hit me. I'm going to give you some space.”

Keep your words simple and focus on creating a safe environment.

If your toddler is in a safe environment, allow the intensity of the moment to settle before trying to teach a bigger lesson.

You don't need to win an argument with a toddler in the middle of a meltdown.

Should I Put My Toddler In Time-Out?

A separate safe space can sometimes be useful when you need to create physical distance or everyone needs a moment to calm down.

But the goal shouldn't be making your child feel rejected or frightened.

❌ THINK LESS ABOUT

“You hit, so you are being punished.”

💗 THINK MORE ABOUT

“Hitting isn't safe, so I'm stopping the interaction and helping everyone calm down.”

Once your toddler is calm, you can reconnect and teach the alternative behaviour.

What Should I Avoid Doing?

When you're being hit repeatedly, it's completely understandable to feel angry.

But some responses can make the situation harder or send a confusing message.

❌ Hitting Back

This can send a confusing message: “Hitting is wrong, except when an adult does it.”

❌ Long Lectures

A toddler who is extremely upset probably isn't ready to process a long explanation. Keep the immediate response short.

❌ Repeating Yourself Endlessly

You don't need to say “no hitting” 25 times. Set the boundary, then act on it.

❌ Huge Threats

Keep consequences realistic and predictable instead of making threats you may not follow through with.

❌ Forcing An Apology Immediately

A forced “sorry” doesn't necessarily teach the skill you actually want your toddler to develop. Once they're calm, you can help them repair the situation in a meaningful way.

What Can I Teach My Toddler Instead?

Toddlers need alternatives.

If hitting happens because they're frustrated, give them simple words they can eventually learn to use.

🗣️ “Stop.”
🙋 “Help me.”
🚫 “I don't like that.”
🔄 “My turn.”
💗 “I'm mad.”
🤲 “Help.”

You can also model physical alternatives.

  • “You can stomp your feet.”
  • “You can squeeze this pillow.”
  • “You can come get Mommy.”
  • “You can say ‘help.’”

They won't necessarily use these alternatives perfectly the first time. You're teaching a skill that takes practice.

What If My Toddler Hits Completely Out Of Nowhere?

Sometimes there really doesn't seem to be an obvious trigger.

That doesn't mean you have failed to figure out some hidden problem. Toddlers can have very fast-moving impulses.

One moment everything is fine. The next moment they're hitting.

Instead of spending all your energy trying to find the perfect explanation, focus on what you can control:

Stop the hitting → Create safety → Stay calm → Reconnect → Teach the alternative

You can also start noticing patterns over several days.

Does it happen more often when your toddler is:

  • Hungry?
  • Tired?
  • Transitioning between activities?
  • Told “no”?
  • Asked to share?
  • Competing for your attention?
  • Overstimulated?
  • Frustrated because they can't communicate something?

Patterns can give you clues about what might be making the behaviour more likely.

💗 What To Do After Your Toddler Calms Down

This is where some of the most important teaching can happen.

You don't need a huge conversation. Keep it simple.

TRY THIS

“You were really angry. You wanted Mommy's attention. You hit me. I won't let you hit. Next time you can say ‘Mommy, help me.’”

Then reconnect.

If someone was hurt, you can also involve your toddler in an age-appropriate repair:

“Let's check if Mommy is okay.”

The focus isn't on shame. It's on learning:

I had a big feeling → I hit → The hitting was stopped → I can use another way next time

🧸 A Simple 4-Step Response To Remember

When you're exhausted and your toddler is hitting, you don't need to remember a parenting textbook.

Remember these four steps:

1

STOP

Stop the hitting and protect yourself and anyone else nearby.

2

SET THE BOUNDARY

“I won't let you hit me.”

3

CREATE SPACE

Move away or help your toddler move to a safe space if needed.

4

TEACH LATER

Once they're calm, practice what they can do instead.

Safety first. Teaching second.

What If Nothing Seems To Work?

Behaviour doesn't always change after one perfect response. Toddlers learn through repetition.

You may need to respond to the same behaviour many times before the alternative behaviour becomes easier for them.

If the hitting is frequent, severe, causing injuries, happening across many settings, or you're concerned about your child's development or behaviour more generally, it's reasonable to bring it up with your child's doctor or another qualified child-development professional.

You don't have to wait until you're completely overwhelmed.

Asking for guidance is part of parenting too.

💗

You Are Not Failing Because Your Toddler Hits

One of the hardest parts of toddler behaviour is how personal it can feel. Especially when you're the person they're hitting.

It can make you wonder:

“Why are they doing this to me?”

But your toddler's behaviour isn't a measurement of your worth as a parent.

You can be a loving, attentive parent and still have a toddler who hits.

Your job isn't to make sure your toddler never experiences anger or frustration. Your job is to help them gradually learn what to do with those feelings without hurting people.

And that takes time. 💗
