Activities for Infants Simple Play Ideas for 0–12 Months, Band by Band

Finding activities for infants 0-12 months that match what your baby can actually do is the whole trick — too young and it washes over them, too old and they crawl off mid-game. This page organises simple, free play ideas into four age bands, 0–3, 4–6, 7–9 and 10–12 months, so you can start where your baby is today and keep going all the way to the first birthday.

Last updated: November 5, 2026

Quick Answer

Match the activity to the band: faces, voices, contrast cards and tummy time for 0–3 months; reaching, grasping and rolling play for 4–6; hiding, banging, filling and dumping for 7–9; and posting, stacking, ball rolling and first pretend play for 10–12 months. Short sessions in awake windows, on the floor, with things from home — that is the whole system.

👶 How to Use This Page

Activities for infants 0-12 months change fast, because babies do. The game that delights a 3-month-old — a face, very close, pulling expressions — is of no use to a crawling 9-month-old, and a 9-month-old's posting box would baffle a newborn. So this page is organised into four age bands: 0–3, 4–6, 7–9 and 10–12 months, with four or five short, real activities in each.

Use the bands as a rough map, not a test. Babies reach each stage across a wide range of normal timing, and plenty of activities work a band earlier or later than listed. The Montessori principle applies at every age: follow your baby. Offer something, watch the response, and let interest — not the chart — decide how long it lasts. Every activity here is for awake, supervised floor play with things you already have at home.

A quick note: this page is general play information, not medical or developmental advice. If your baby was born early, use their adjusted age as a guide and check with your pediatrician about what suits them.

🍼 0–3 Months: First Play Is Faces and Voices

A newborn's world is close-up and simple: your face at feeding distance, your voice, strong patterns, gentle touch and short bursts of tummy time. That is not a limitation — it is the whole programme.

  • Face-to-face chat. Hold your baby 20–30 cm (8–12 inches) from your face, talk, smile, raise your eyebrows — then pause and wait. Young babies answer slowly, with looks, wiggles and, from about six weeks, smiles. Builds bonding, vision and early communication.
  • High-contrast cards. Show bold black-and-white cards or a patterned board book at feeding distance and move it slowly sideways so your baby's eyes follow. Young eyes see strong contrast clearest. Builds focus and eye tracking.
  • Tummy time, little and often. Start on your reclined chest for a minute or two, then short floor sessions on a firm mat, ending while it is still going well. Builds the neck, shoulder and arm strength everything else depends on.
  • Singing and humming. Sing anything, hum against your baby's chest while you hold them, narrate the changing and feeding. Your voice is the most familiar sound in their world. Builds hearing and the foundations of language.
  • Texture touch. Stroke soft fabrics — muslin, fleece, ribbed cotton — slowly over hands, feet and cheeks, and let fingers close around them. Builds touch awareness and grasping. Play-only: cloths go away afterwards, never into the sleep space.

🌱 4–6 Months: Reaching, Grasping and Rolling

Hands open up and the world becomes grabbable. Play is now about reaching for things, getting them to the mouth, and discovering that rolling goes somewhere interesting.

  • Reach-and-grasp play. Lay your baby on their back or tummy and hold or place a large, baby-safe toy just within reach — close enough to succeed, far enough to stretch for. Builds arm strength, hand-eye coordination and determination.
  • Rolling encouragement. During floor play, put a favourite toy just to one side so your baby turns their head, shifts their weight and reaches across their body toward it. That twist is how rolls begin. Never force a roll — let your baby do the moving.
  • Peekaboo. The original disappearing game: hide your face behind your hands or a muslin for two seconds, then reappear. Builds early object permanence — the idea that things still exist when hidden — plus giggles.
  • Rattle and crinkle play. Offer a soft rattle or crinkle toy, shake it beside your baby, then let them hold, shake and mouth it themselves. Use baby-safe, cleanable toys only, and put them away after play.
  • Bath-time water play. Supported in the bath, let your baby splash, kick and watch you pour water from a cup in a slow stream. Builds body awareness and cause and effect. Never leave a baby alone in water, even for a moment.

🐾 7–9 Months: Finding, Banging and Moving

Sitting, crawling or both are arriving, and play gets physical: things hidden must be found, things held must be banged, and the far side of the room must be reached.

  • Hidden toy hunt. While your baby watches, tuck a favourite toy partly under a cloth, then fully under it. Let them pull the cloth away and find it. Builds object permanence and problem solving — and it stays funny for months.
  • Bang-it-together time. Two blocks, two lids, a wooden spoon and a pot: babies this age are serious percussionists. Builds arm control, cause and effect and early rhythm.
  • Fill and dump. A box or basket with a few large, safe objects — big soft blocks, large balls, chunky rings. Your baby fills it, tips it out, and starts again. Everything must be too big to choke on: if it fits inside a toilet-paper tube, it stays out.
  • Cushion mountain. A low course of sofa cushions and pillows on the floor to crawl over, around and into, with you right there. Builds crawling strength, balance and confidence. Keep it low, soft and supervised.
  • Babble back and forth. When your baby babbles, answer in kind — copy their sounds, pause, and let them go again. These turn-taking 'conversations' are real language play, and they work during any other activity on this page.

🎂 10–12 Months: Posting, Stacking and First Pretend

Nearly one, and play turns purposeful: things go into containers on purpose, towers exist to be demolished, and a toy phone is serious business.

  • Posting box. Cut a wide slot in a box lid (an adult job) and give your baby large balls or chunky cards to post through it. Open, retrieve, repeat. Builds hand control, aim and persistence. Check homemade edges are smooth and taped.
  • Stack and knock down. Big soft or wooden blocks: you stack two or three, your baby demolishes them, then stacking attempts begin. Builds fine motor control and an excellent understanding of gravity.
  • Roll the ball back. Sit facing each other, legs apart, and roll a large soft ball slowly between you. Builds turn-taking, tracking and the social rhythm of a game with another person.
  • Cruising and push-along play. For babies pulling up: arrange sturdy, stable furniture so they can cruise along it, and offer a heavy, stable push-along toy on flat floor. Supervise closely and skip anything light or wheeled that can shoot away from them.
  • Simple pretend. A toy cup to 'drink' from, a toy phone to 'talk' into, a doll to pat. Copy your baby's pretend actions and add one of your own. First pretend play is the start of imagination — and it needs no special toys at all.

⚠️ Safety First — the Rules That Run Through Every Band

  • Awake and supervised. Every activity here is for awake play with an adult present and watching. Floor play is not a moment to leave the room.
  • The toilet-paper tube test. If an object fits completely inside a toilet-paper tube, it is a choking risk for under-3s and stays out of baby play. Check toys regularly for damage and loose parts.
  • Play is awake time; sleep is back time. Activities end when your baby is tired. Babies sleep on their backs, in an empty crib or bassinet — no toys, cloths or play items in the sleep space.
  • Water means hands on. Never leave a baby alone with water — bath, bowl or tray — not even briefly.
  • Follow your baby. Turning away, fussing, arching or going drowsy means stop. An activity your baby is done with has done its job.
How to use

🔁 How to Use These Activities Day to Day

  1. Find your baby's band. Start with age, then adjust by what your baby is actually doing — sitting, crawling, cruising — rather than by the calendar alone.
  2. Offer one activity at a time. A few well-chosen things on the floor beat a pile of toys. Rotate across days, not within the hour.
  3. Protect floor time. The single most useful habit on this page: regular time on a firm floor, free to move, with you nearby. Most activities here grow out of it.
  4. Stop on the first cue. Hungry, tired or fed up means the session is over, however good the activity was going.
  5. Revisit old favourites. Babies love repetition — peekaboo at 5 months and peekaboo at 11 months are both correct. Retire activities when interest genuinely fades, not before.

FAQ

❓ Activities for Infants FAQ

What activities can I do with my infant?

Match the activity to the age band. Young infants need faces, voices, high-contrast patterns and tummy time. From about 4 months, add reaching, grasping and rolling play. From 7 months, babies enjoy finding hidden objects, banging, filling and dumping. By 10–12 months, stacking, posting games, rolling a ball back and forth and early pretend play all land well. Every band on this page lists activities for infants that are genuinely doable at that age.

How much playtime does a baby need each day?

There is no required amount. Babies learn from the whole day — feeds, baths, being carried, watching the household — and short play sessions of a few minutes at a time, fitted into awake windows, are plenty. Lots of floor time across the day, where your baby is free to move, matters more than any single activity.

What are the best activities for a 6 month old?

At around 6 months most babies enjoy reaching for and grasping toys, rolling toward something interesting, peekaboo, splashing at bath time, and banging and mouthing safe objects. Sit-supported floor play with a few large, cleanable toys is the everyday staple; the 4–6 month band on this page lists the full set.

When do babies start playing with toys?

Gradually. Newborns 'play' mostly with faces and voices. Bat-and-grasp play starts across the first few months, purposeful shaking and banging around the middle of the first year, and by the end of the first year many babies are stacking, posting and rolling toys back and forth. Babies reach these steps across a wide range of normal timing.

Are these activities okay for a premature baby?

Use your baby's adjusted age (counting from their due date) as your guide for which band fits, and — more importantly — ask your pediatrician or health visitor what suits your baby, especially in the early months. They know your baby's start in life and can advise better than any webpage.

What if my baby doesn't like an activity?

Try something else, or the same thing another day. Disliking tummy time at first, ignoring a toy, or lasting thirty seconds at an activity are all completely normal. Follow your baby: the right activity is the one in front of a contented baby, and stopping is always an acceptable outcome. If you are ever worried about your baby's development, mention it to your pediatrician.
