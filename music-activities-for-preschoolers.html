Music Activities for Preschoolers 14 Easy Rhythm, Dance and Singing Games

Music activities for preschoolers do not need instruments, lessons or a musical bone in your body — they need a song, some kitchen supplies and room to move. This page gives you 14 real ones: freeze dance, homemade shakers, copy-my-clap, a kitchen band, sound matching and more, each with a quick setup, clear how-to-play steps and the skills it builds.

Last updated: November 5, 2026

Quick Answer

Freeze dance is the fastest win: play a song, dance, freeze when it stops. After that, homemade shakers (a sealed pot of rice), copy-my-clap rhythm games and marching action songs cover most days. Every activity here starts in under five minutes, costs nothing beyond household supplies, and runs five to fifteen minutes — or until the dancing collapses into giggling, which also counts.

🎶 Why Music Activities Punch Above Their Weight

Music play looks like pure fun, and it is — but look at what a preschooler is actually doing during it. They are listening with real attention, holding patterns in memory, counting beats without knowing they are counting, moving a whole body in time, and taking turns leading and following. Music activities for preschoolers bundle listening, maths patterns, language and movement into the thing children would choose to do anyway.

None of the fourteen activities here needs a real instrument, a musical grown-up or a quiet house (the neighbours point is negotiable). Everything uses voices, hands and kitchen supplies, every activity starts in under five minutes, and each one lists what you need, how to play and what it builds. Start with freeze dance — it is the one they will ask for tomorrow.

🧊 1. Freeze Dance

The all-time most requested preschool music game, and the simplest: dance while the music plays, freeze when it stops.

Setup: A phone or speaker and any upbeat song. Clear a space with room to move.

How to play: Play the music and everyone dances. Stop it without warning — everyone freezes in whatever pose they are in. Hold the freeze for a few seconds, then start the music again. Take turns being the person in charge of stopping the music.

Skills: Listening, self-control, balance and gross motor skills — freezing mid-move is real body work.

🥁 2. Homemade Shakers

Making the instrument is half the activity. A sealed pot of something rattly becomes a shaker your child built themselves.

Setup: An adult fills a small screw-top pot or lidded container with a tablespoon of rice, dried pasta or beads, and seals it tight — hot glue or strong tape on the lid for younger children. Check the seal before every use.

How to play: Put on a favourite song and shake along. Try shaking fast and slow, loud and quiet, high and low. Between songs, compare shakers: different fillings genuinely sound different, and children love ranking them.

Skills: Rhythm, cause and effect, fine motor control and early comparing and sorting.

👏 3. Copy-My-Clap Rhythm Games

No supplies, no setup, works anywhere: you clap a pattern, your child claps it back.

Setup: Just hands. Sit facing each other.

How to play: Start with two claps. Then clap–clap–pause–clap. Build slowly to four- and five-beat patterns, mixing claps with thigh pats and stomps. When your child is ready, swap roles — copying a preschooler's pattern faithfully is harder, and funnier, than it looks.

Skills: Listening, memory, pattern recognition and turn-taking.

🧣 4. Dancing With Scarves

Something light to wave makes slow music visible. Scarves, tissues or squares of fabric turn tempo into movement children can see.

Setup: One scarf, tissue or lightweight fabric square per dancer. Works best with music that changes speed or mood.

How to play: Dance holding the scarves and let them lead: swirl them high for loud parts, drift them low and slow for quiet parts, toss and catch on a big finish (tissues float down slowly enough to catch). Call out changes — 'the music is sneaking… now it's stomping!'.

Skills: Gross motor control, responding to tempo and dynamics, and creative expression.

🎤 5. Sound Matching Pairs

A listening game with a real puzzle in it: pairs of shakers that sound the same, hidden among ones that don't.

Setup: An adult prepares pairs of identical small sealed containers — two with rice, two with pasta, two with a few coins, two with sand or sugar — sealed tightly. Start with two pairs for younger children, up to four pairs for older preschoolers.

How to play: Shake one container, then hunt through the others, shaking each, to find its matching sound. Play it as a memory game face down for an extra challenge, and always let the finder open the guessing — 'that one sounds crunchy, like mine'.

Skills: Focused listening, memory and early classification — telling sounds apart is a real pre-reading skill.

🚶 6. March and Move Action Songs

Songs that tell you what to do remove all the planning: the lyrics are the instructions.

Setup: Any action song your family knows — the kind that has children clapping, stomping, turning around, reaching up and freezing on cue. Sing it yourselves or play a recording.

How to play: Everyone does the action the song names, when it names it. Sing it a second time faster. Older preschoolers can take over giving extra instructions between verses: 'now hop!', 'now tiptoe!'.

Skills: Following directions, vocabulary, whole-body movement and timing.

🥄 7. Kitchen Band Parade

Every kitchen contains a band waiting to happen. Pots are drums, wooden spoons are sticks, and the hallway is a parade route.

Setup: Upturned pots, boxes or mixing bowls, wooden spoons, a whisk against a cooling rack — anything sturdy that makes a sound when tapped. Agree one house rule first: instruments are tapped, not thrown.

How to play: Start a marching beat and parade around the room, taking turns leading. The leader sets the speed and everyone matches. Finish with a proper parade wave past an audience of stuffed animals.

Skills: Rhythm-keeping, cooperation, and the beginning of playing in time with other people.

🎹 8. High–Low, Loud–Quiet

A call-and-response game that teaches the words music is described with — by making children's bodies do them.

Setup: A grown-up voice, a piano or keyboard app, or any instrument. No other supplies.

How to play: Make a high sound — children stretch up tall on tiptoes. A low sound — they crouch down small. Loud — stomp. Quiet — tiptoe. Start slowly and predictably, then mix it up and speed it up. Swap roles and let your child conduct you.

Skills: Listening discrimination, musical vocabulary and whole-body control.

🪑 9. Musical Cushions (Nobody's Out)

Musical chairs without the tears: when the music stops, everyone finds a cushion — and sharing one counts as winning.

Setup: One cushion or floor mat per child to start, scattered on the floor. Music you can stop and start.

How to play: Dance or march around the cushions while the music plays. When it stops, everyone sits on a cushion. Each round, quietly remove one cushion — now two children share. Keep going until the whole group piles giggling onto the last one. Nobody is ever out; squeezing in is the game.

Skills: Listening, quick reactions, cooperation and sharing space kindly.

📦 10. Box Drum Circle

Boxes and buckets make deep, satisfying drums, and a circle makes taking turns natural.

Setup: One box, bucket or upturned bowl per player, hands or wooden spoons as beaters. Sit in a circle.

How to play: One person plays a simple beat; the circle copies it. Pass the leader role around. Try a 'wave': each person plays one beat in turn around the circle, keeping the pulse going. Finish with everyone drumming together as loudly as the neighbours allow.

Skills: Turn-taking, keeping a steady beat, listening to a group and confidence to lead.

🔔 11. Guess That Sound

A blanket, a few household noisemakers and a mystery to solve — this quiet game is a favourite after lunch.

Setup: A blanket or screen to hide behind, and sound-making objects: keys, a spoon in a cup, crinkly paper, a zip, a squeaky toy, rice poured into a bowl.

How to play: Hide behind the blanket and make one sound. Children guess what made it. Correct guessers can take a turn being the sound-maker. For older preschoolers, make two sounds in a row and ask for the order.

Skills: Listening attention, memory and vocabulary for describing sounds.

🎵 12. Sing-and-Stop Songs

Take a song everyone knows by heart and break it on purpose: stop mid-line and let the children land the ending.

Setup: Nothing but voices. Works best with a short, very familiar song — a nursery rhyme your child could sing in their sleep.

How to play: Sing together, then the grown-up stops suddenly just before the last word of a line. Children shout or sing the missing word. As they get good, stop earlier, stop in the middle of a word, or mouth the words silently and let them carry the song alone.

Skills: Memory, anticipation, language and the building blocks of reading rhythm.

🌧️ 13. Rainmaker Bottles and Weather Sounds

A sealed bottle of rice, tipped slowly, sounds astonishingly like rain — and a whole weather system can be built from body sounds.

Setup: An adult seals rice or small pasta inside a clear bottle with a screw lid, taped or glued shut. That is the rainmaker.

How to play: Tip the bottle slowly for rain, fast for a downpour. Build a storm together: rub palms for wind, pat thighs for rain, tap fingers for drizzle, stomp for thunder — a grown-up narrates the weather and the children make it. End every storm with the sun coming out and everyone still and quiet.

Skills: Dynamics (loud and quiet), storytelling and group timing.

💃 14. Mirror Dancing — Copy the Dancer

One dancer, one mirror. The simplest dance game there is, and the one that produces the most inventive moves.

Setup: Music with a clear beat and space for two people to face each other.

How to play: Stand face to face. The leader dances; the mirror copies every move as exactly as they can. Swap roles every song, or every verse for fast changers. Mirroring a slow-motion leader is a hilarious challenge for older preschoolers — and genuinely hard.

Skills: Observation, body awareness, empathy and creative movement.

How to use

🔁 How to Use Music Activities Week to Week

  1. Use music as a movement break. Ten minutes of freeze dance between quiet activities burns off exactly the energy that makes sitting still hard.
  2. Repeat favourites shamelessly. Preschoolers learn by repetition — the tenth round of copy-my-clap is where the patterns get interesting, not boring.
  3. End with a calm-down song. A slow song, scarves drifting, or the end of a weather storm brings the energy down gently instead of cutting the fun off.
  4. Let children lead. The child who stops the music, leads the drum circle or conducts high–low is doing the deepest learning on the page.
  5. Keep instruments sealed and checked. Homemade shakers and rainmakers are adult-made: lids glued or taped, seals checked before use, and any split container binned straight away.

FAQ

❓ Music Activities for Preschoolers FAQ

What are good music activities for preschoolers?

Freeze dance, homemade shakers, copy-my-clap rhythm games, dancing with scarves, a kitchen band and sound matching games are all excellent music activities for preschoolers. The best ones need no real instruments, start in under five minutes, and let children move, listen and make noise in roughly equal measure.

Do preschoolers need real instruments for music activities?

No. A sealed container of rice is a shaker, a wooden spoon on an upturned box is a drum, and hands are the original rhythm instrument. Real child-sized instruments are lovely if you have them, but every activity on this page works with things from the kitchen and recycling box.

How do music activities help preschoolers learn?

Music play gives children practice in listening closely, copying patterns, counting beats without realising it, and moving their bodies in time and in space. Singing together also exercises memory and language — children memorise long songs word-perfectly long before they can memorise a shopping list. Treat it as play that carries learning along with it, not a lesson in disguise.

How long should a music activity last?

Five to fifteen minutes suits most preschoolers, and shorter is fine. Music play is energetic; many families use it as a movement break between quieter activities. Follow the energy — when children start wandering off or the noise tips from joyful to frazzled, it is time for a calm song to finish.

What if my child won't join in?

Let them watch. Watching, hovering at the edge and joining for the last thirty seconds are all normal ways in. Keep invitations light, never force a turn, and try a different role — the person who starts and stops the music for freeze dance holds the most powerful job in the room and shy children often love it.

Can toddlers join these music activities?

Most of them, with small changes. Toddlers can freeze dance (expect approximate freezing), shake shakers, march and dance with scarves. Skip the small-parts instruments, keep sound matching to two very different sounds, and let toddlers copy in their own way rather than asking them to match a pattern exactly.
