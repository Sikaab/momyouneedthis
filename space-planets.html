How To Use This Printable

  1. Download and print the space and planets chart on letter-size paper.
  2. Hang it at your child's eye level — a chart on a wall teaches all week; a chart in a drawer teaches once.
  3. Walk the order together — count the planets out from the Sun, naming each, until your child can run the parade solo.
  4. Play the lookup game — take turns asking questions only the chart can answer: “Which planet is red?” “Which two are blue?” “Which is smallest?” Looking it up is the game.

Why Learn About Space & Planets?

Space is the first topic that teaches scale: a child learns their address is a street, in a town, on a planet, in a system of eight, around one ordinary star among billions. That widening — handled gently, with a chart on the wall — is wonder with a structure, and it reliably produces the question-asking years.

The chart format builds early science vocabulary (planet, orbit, star, solar system) and the reference habit at once. It also quietly prepares the ground for the deeper space pages on this site — astronauts, the Moon, the stars — each of which zooms into one square of this chart.

🪐 Reading The Planet Chart

Order is the chart's backbone: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune — always counted out from the Sun, because distance from the Sun is what decides each planet's temperature and year length. Mercury's year is 88 days; Neptune's is 165 Earth years. Nobody has ever had a birthday on Neptune.

Two families: the four inner planets are small and rocky — worlds with ground. The four outer giants are enormous balls of gas and liquid with no surface to stand on. The chart's size jump between Mars and Jupiter is the whole story at a glance.

Colors are clues: Mars is red from rusty dust; Neptune is deep blue; Venus is creamy yellow-white under its clouds; Earth is the blue marble, colored by oceans — the only planet whose color is mostly water, and the only one with anybody on it looking at charts.

Two chart questions every child asks: Is the Sun a planet? No — it is a star, and the planets are its family, held by its gravity. Is the Moon a planet? No — it is Earth's moon, a satellite: a world that orbits a planet instead of the Sun. Both answers are on the chart's logic, and a child who can give them owns the map.
