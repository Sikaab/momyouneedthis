Newborn Exercises: Gentle Moves That Help Your Baby Grow Strong

Search for newborn exercises and you will find everything from baby gyms to alarming milestone charts. The truth is quieter: a newborn's exercise is short, gentle floor play — tummy time, kicking, gripping, bicycling legs — led by your baby and finished the moment they've had enough. Here is what actually helps, how to do each move, and when to check in with your pediatrician instead.

By Jessika • 💗 MomYouNeedThis • Practical Parenting Guide • Published November 5, 2026
First, remember: newborn exercise is play, not training. 👶

Your baby is not behind, does not need a workout plan, and will never once need to be pushed. What young babies do need is plenty of chances to move freely on the floor — and a few gentle games that make that movement fun for both of you.

Everything in this guide is soft, short and baby-led. If your baby protests, the exercise is over, and that is the exercise done right.

💡 The Quick Answer

The newborn exercises that matter are simple: tummy time — on your chest first, then the floor — plus gentle bicycle legs, something safe to grip, and chances to kick against your hands. A few minutes at a time, in your baby's calm awake windows, is plenty. Rolling practice comes later and grows out of side-lying play, not drills.

🐛
Tummy time is the main event.

If you do only one thing from this page, make it short, frequent tummy time. It builds the neck, shoulder and arm strength that rolling, sitting and crawling are all built on.

A quick safety frame before the moves themselves: always support your baby's head, keep movements slow and gentle, never shake or bounce a baby roughly, and stop the moment your baby has clearly had enough. Exercises happen on the floor or on you — never on a raised surface your baby could roll or slide from.

🐛 Tummy Time — Where It All Starts

Tummy time is the closest thing a newborn has to a full-body workout. Lifting that heavy head against gravity strengthens the neck, shoulders, arms and back, and time spent on the front also gives the back of the head a break from lying flat.

Start on your chest. In the first days and weeks, recline at about 45 degrees and lay your baby tummy-down on your chest. They will lift and turn their head to find your face and voice — that turning and lifting is the exercise. A minute or two is a complete session.

Move to the floor. Use a firm mat or clean blanket on the floor, forearms tucked under your baby's shoulders. A small rolled towel under the chest can help early on. Get down at eye level, put your face or a high-contrast card in front of them, and finish while it is still going well — ending on a good note is how sessions gradually get longer.

Little and often wins. A few short sessions spread across the day beat one long, unhappy one. Fit them after naps and changes, when your baby is awake and content, and wait a while after a big feed so a full tummy isn't pressed into the floor.

🚲 Bicycle Legs

Bicycle legs are exactly what they sound like: your baby lies on their back while you gently move their legs in a slow, soft cycling motion, as if pedalling an invisible bicycle.

How to do it: Hold your baby's lower legs gently, one in each hand, and move them in slow circles — knees bending and straightening in turn. Keep the movement small and easy, and never push a leg further or straighter than it goes on its own. Thirty seconds to a minute is plenty.

Parents often use bicycle legs when a baby seems gassy and uncomfortable, as the gentle movement can help things move along. Treat it as a soothing game rather than a treatment: if your baby relaxes and passes wind, lovely; if they stiffen or cry, stop and try a cuddle instead.

🤲 Grasp Practice — Something to Hold On To

Newborns are born with a grasp reflex: touch a palm and those tiny fingers close around whatever is there. Grasp play turns that reflex into the beginnings of real hand skills.

Try this: stroke your clean finger into your baby's palm and let them grip it, then gently tug — just enough for them to feel their own hold. Offer a soft, baby-safe toy or a rolled muslin cloth against the palm the same way. As the weeks pass, hold a toy where your baby can see it and brush it against the back of their hand to encourage the swiping and reaching that comes next.

Everything offered for grasping should be big enough not to be a choking risk, soft, clean, and put away after play — grasp toys never go into the crib or bassinet.

🔄 Rolling Prep — Side-to-Side Play

Babies do not learn to roll from being rolled. They learn it from weeks of tummy time, head turning and reaching — and from playing on their sides, the halfway position rolling passes through.

Side-lying play: during floor play, gently roll your baby onto one side with a rolled towel behind their back for support, and put a toy or your face where they can see it. Play for a short while, then do the other side. Both sides, every time — it keeps head-turning and body use balanced.

Reaching across: as your baby grows toward 3–4 months, place a toy slightly to one side during tummy time or back-lying play so they turn their head, shift their weight and reach across their body toward it. That twist is the seed of a roll.

Helping a roll they started: once your baby is pushing up strongly and twisting with real intent, you can gently guide the last little bit of a roll they began — supporting a hip, never forcing. If your baby is not there yet, they are not late; rolling arrives across a wide range of normal timing.

🦶 Kicking Games

Newborn legs are busy. Given something to push against, many babies will kick, pause, and kick again — clearly pleased with the effect.

Lay your baby on their back and hold your palms against the soles of their feet so they can push. Talk or sing while they kick. A crinkle mat or foil blanket held firmly at foot level (supervised, and away from the face) gives the kicks a satisfying sound. Kicking builds leg strength and an early sense of cause and effect: I move, something happens.

⏱️ How Much, How Often?

Think in minutes, not schedules. In the early weeks, a minute or two of tummy time a few times a day, bicycle legs when your baby seems gassy, and grasp and kicking play folded into ordinary floor time is a full programme. As your baby grows, sessions naturally stretch — led entirely by how long your baby stays happy.

The pattern that works for most families: play in the calm, alert window after a feed has settled and a change is done. Skip formal play when your baby is hungry, overtired or unwell. Ordinary life — being carried, lying on the floor watching the household go by, baths, cuddles — is movement and learning too.

Follow your baby, not the clock. Turning away, hiccups, arching, fussing or a drowsy glaze are all ways of saying "enough". Stopping early keeps play something your baby looks forward to.

🛑 When to Stop — and When to Ask Your Pediatrician

Stop any exercise straight away if your baby cries and does not settle with a pause and a cuddle, arches strongly away, goes pale or dusky, vomits, or simply resists a movement. Never force a limb, never continue through clear distress, and never shake or roughly bounce a baby. If a movement ever seems to hurt, do not repeat it.

Ask your pediatrician for guidance — sooner rather than later — if:

  • Your baby seems very stiff, or very floppy, compared with what you expected
  • Your baby strongly prefers looking one way only, or has a flattened spot developing on one side of the head
  • Your baby is not lifting their head at all in tummy time after the early weeks
  • Your baby loses a movement skill they had before
  • Your baby was born prematurely or has any medical condition, before adding new exercises
  • You are worried about anything at all — parental instinct is a valid reason to ask

Most babies develop across a wide, healthy range of timing, and most worries turn out to be normal variation. Your pediatrician knows your baby and is the right person to tell the difference — this page cannot, and it is not trying to.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

What Do Parents Ask About Newborn Exercises?

Quick answers to the questions parents ask most about this topic.

What are the best newborn exercises?

Tummy time is the main one — short sessions on your chest, then on the floor, a few times a day. After that, gentle bicycle legs, giving your baby something to grip, and letting them kick against your hands cover most of what a newborn needs. All newborn exercises should be gentle, brief and baby-led: this is play, not training.

When can I start doing exercises with my newborn?

Gentle play can start in the first days — tummy time on a caregiver's chest is the usual first 'exercise'. Keep everything very short at the start, work around feeds and naps, and follow your baby's cues. If your baby was born early or has any medical condition, ask your pediatrician before starting anything beyond everyday handling and cuddles.

How much tummy time does a newborn need?

Start with a minute or two at a time, a few times a day, and build gradually as your baby tolerates it — many short sessions work better than one long battle. Most babies protest at first; ending while it's still going well and trying again later is how tummy time grows. There is no single magic number, and your pediatrician can advise for your baby.

Are bicycle legs safe for newborns?

Yes, when done gently: support your baby's legs and move them slowly in a soft cycling motion, never forcing a leg straighter or further than it goes easily. Many parents use bicycle legs to help a gassy baby get comfortable. Stop if your baby stiffens, cries or resists, and avoid firm pressure on the tummy right after a feed.

What exercises help a baby learn to roll over?

Rolling is something babies grow into rather than something to teach. Side-lying play on both sides, tummy time, and placing a toy just to the side so your baby turns their head and reaches across their body all build the strength and movement patterns rolling uses. As your baby starts pushing and twisting on their own — often around 3 to 4 months — you can gently help the last part of a roll they started. Never force a roll.

When should I talk to a pediatrician about my baby's movement?

Any time you are worried — that is reason enough. In particular, mention it if your baby seems very stiff or very floppy, strongly prefers turning their head one way only, isn't lifting their head at all during tummy time after the early weeks, or loses a movement skill they previously had. Your pediatrician knows your baby and can tell you what is normal variation and what deserves a closer look.

Quick note: I'm a mom, not a medical professional. This page is general play information only, not medical advice. If you're worried about your baby's movement or development, check with your pediatrician.
