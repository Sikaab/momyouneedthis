How To Use This Printable

Download and print the pattern page. Work one row at a time: say the shapes out loud together, pointing at each one — “circle, square, circle, square…” — and clap or tap the table on the beat if it helps the pair feel like a rhythm.

When the chant reaches the empty box, stop and ask what shape comes next. The answer is the shape that starts the unit again. Let your child draw it in the box — a wobbly circle counts fully; the pattern thinking is the exercise, and the drawing is how the answer gets recorded.

Row by row, the pairs change: triangle–star, heart–diamond, oval–rectangle, hexagon–circle, star–triangle. If a row goes wrong, chant it again from the start rather than naming the right shape — hearing where the rhythm breaks teaches more than being told.

Why Learn Shape Patterns?

Shape patterns ask more than color patterns do. A color can be pointed at; a shape must be recognized — its sides, corners and curves identified — before it can take its turn in the sequence. Running an AB pattern in shapes therefore practices shape knowledge and pattern logic in the same motion, each skill checking the other.

The deeper prize is the unit idea. A child who can look at circle–square–circle–square and see “the pair repeats” has grasped that patterns are built from a chunk that recurs — the exact idea underneath counting by twos, calendar weeks, and every repeating structure math will ever show them. Drawing the next shape makes the prediction physical: the hand commits to what the mind foresaw.

📋 The Six Rows — What Goes In Each Box (Parent’s Key)

Circle – square: the box takes a circle.

Triangle – star: the box takes a triangle.

Heart – diamond: the box takes a heart.

Oval – rectangle: the box takes an oval.

Hexagon – circle: the box takes a hexagon.

Star – triangle: the box takes a star.

Every answer is the row's first shape returning — because an AB pattern that has run twice starts its unit a third time. If your child draws the SECOND shape instead (square after circle–square–circle–square), they are predicting one step too few: they answered “what alternates?” instead of “what comes next in the run?” Chant the row with a finger tapping each shape and the box usually corrects itself.

How do we work on patterns?

Continuing a pattern is the middle step of pattern learning: after recognizing that a pattern repeats, a child learns to extend it — predicting what comes next and committing to the prediction. On this sheet the prediction is a drawn shape, so the page also builds shape recognition and early drawing control. The step after this is completing patterns with gaps in the middle, which asks the child to check the pattern in both directions.
