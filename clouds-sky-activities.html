☁️ The Three Cloud Families

Cumulus — the fluffy piles. White, heaped, cauliflower clouds with flat bottoms. They are fair-weather clouds, the ones that look like animals and ships. If they start growing taller and darker, though, they are changing into storm clouds — the pile is a promise that can be withdrawn.

Cirrus — the wisps. Thin, feathery streaks very high up, made of ice crystals rather than water drops. They often arrive a day before weather changes — old sky-watchers called them mare's tails and read them as a warning to check the forecast.

Stratus — the gray blanket. A flat sheet covering the whole sky, dimming the light. This is the rain-coming cloud: drizzle and steady rain fall from stratus, and fog is simply a stratus cloud that has come down to the ground.

Underneath all three is the one fact that makes clouds make sense: a cloud is water — millions of droplets so tiny they float. Warm air carries invisible water up, the cold high air squeezes it into droplets, and a cloud is born. Your child is not looking at sky-cotton; they are looking at the water cycle, mid-journey.
